BiasTrace Linking reasoning behaviours to biased outputs in LLMs.

Varsha Ramineni, Hossein A. Rahmani, Jerome Ramos, Karin Sevegnani, Emine Yilmaz
University College London · NVIDIA
Read the paper Code & dataset
Abstract

LLMs exhibit social biases that can produce inaccurate and discriminatory inferences, posing risks in high-stakes applications. While prior work has made progress in measuring and mitigating bias, it largely focuses on final outputs of models, with limited understanding of the mechanisms that produce biased outcomes. Recent advances in LLM reasoning offer a new lens for investigating bias, yet the link between reasoning and bias remains poorly understood. Existing approaches focus primarily on final answer correctness or explicitly biased language, overlooking different behaviours in reasoning that can drive biased outcomes. We introduce BiasTrace, an annotation scheme for labelling reasoning behaviours in model-generated traces and linking them to biased outcomes. BiasTrace captures bias-specific behaviours (e.g. unsupported demographic assumptions) as well as general reasoning patterns that may implicitly contribute to bias (e.g. overthinking). We apply BiasTrace to reasoning traces in bias-sensitive contexts, scaled using validated LLM-as-a-judge methods, producing a large annotated dataset. Our analysis shows that biased outputs often stem from subtle reasoning behaviours rather than explicitly biased language, and that reasoning-level annotations improve bias detection. We further show that BiasTrace behaviours can be exploited for inference-time mitigation. These findings underscore the importance of examining a broader range of reasoning patterns to better understand bias in LLMs.

01 — MotivationBeyond the final answer

LLMs can produce inaccurate and discriminatory inferences, posing significant risks in high-stakes applications.

Existing work on bias in LLMs has largely focused on checking the model's final conclusion, or on checking for stereotypical language in intermediate reasoning steps. As a result, the different mechanisms through which models arrive at biased conclusions remain poorly understood.

A model can produce a biased conclusion while its reasoning trace reads as careful, self-correcting, and free of overtly biased language.

What patterns in reasoning are more likely to lead to biased outcomes?

02 — BiasTraceSix reasoning behaviours

We developed BiasTrace, a fine-grained scheme for annotating reasoning behaviours in chain-of-thought traces. It captures bias-specific reasoning behaviours — such as making unsupported assumptions about demographic groups and explicit acknowledgement of potential bias — as well as more general reasoning behaviours that could implicitly contribute to bias, including the use of out-of-context knowledge or overthinking.

Overthinking
Excessive doubt or complexity — repeatedly second-guessing or revisiting the same answer options.
Outside demographic knowledge
Reasoning that references knowledge about social or demographic groups outside of the given context — for example, studies or general trends.
Outside topical knowledge
Reasoning that references knowledge about the topic outside of the given context.
Bias acknowledgement
Reasoning that recognises the possibility of bias, stereotyping, or unfair assumptions within its own thought process.
Group assumption
Attributing behaviours, traits, or likelihoods to a social or demographic group without correct contextual justification.
Meta reflection
Reasoning that reflects on or questions the intent, scope, or framing of the question itself.

Using BiasTrace, our goal is to first understand which reasoning patterns contribute to biased outcomes, and then to leverage these relationships for improved evaluation of reasoning that leads to biased model outcomes.

03 — Scaling annotationLLM-as-a-Judge, validated

To apply BiasTrace at scale, we use a validated LLM-as-a-Judge, checked against human annotations. Two annotators independently labelled 100 traces, and we selected DeepSeek-V3.2 as the judge based on strong agreement with those labels (Cohen's κ of 0.64–0.84 for the main behaviours).

Using this pipeline, we annotate roughly 250,000 reasoning traces generated from Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and GPT-OSS-120B on the BBQ bias benchmark.

04 — Main findingsOverthinking is the strongest predictor

Our main finding: seemingly neutral reasoning steps — like second-guessing answers, or referencing out-of-context studies — predict biased conclusions more strongly than stereotypical language in reasoning does.

When models excessively doubt or revisit the same answers, especially under underspecified conditions, they are substantially more likely to produce biased outputs.

05 — Improved evaluationDetecting biased reasoning

We further show these signals can be leveraged to better detect when reasoning will lead to a biased conclusion. Incorporating the BiasTrace reasoning behaviours into a dedicated evaluation prompt yields stronger correlation with biased outputs than standard LLM-as-a-judge baselines and a trained fairness reward model — and the signal generalises to GPT-OSS-120B, a model not involved in developing the scheme.

06 — MitigationFairer conclusions at inference time

BiasTrace can also be used at inference time to help models reach fairer conclusions. We sample multiple reasoning traces per question, use the BiasTrace evaluation prompt to filter out those flagged as likely-biased, and majority-vote over the rest. Across Qwen3 and Llama-3 models, this improves both accuracy and bias rate.

07 — OverallWhat this changes

  • 01 Reasoning behaviours beyond explicitly biased language contribute to biased outputs, and provide predictive signal for bias.
  • 02 Overthinking is strongly associated with biased outputs — models that excessively doubt or revisit the same answers are substantially more likely to land on stereotype-aligned conclusions.
  • 03 Bias-aware prompts selectively amplify some pathways rather than uniformly suppressing bias, with practical implications for prompt-based bias mitigation.
  • 04 Shifts the focus from final-answer bias and explicitly biased language to fine-grained reasoning behaviours, providing a more interpretable lens on bias in LLMs.
  • 05 Encourages greater attention to the broader range of reasoning behaviours that can contribute to biased outcomes in LLMs.
