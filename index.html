Title

BiasTrace

Linking reasoning behaviours to biased outputs in large language models

Authors
Varsha Ramineni1, Hossein A. Rahmani1, Jerome Ramos1, Karin Sevegnani2, Emine Yilmaz1
1University College London    2NVIDIA
Compiled
17 August 2026 · v0.3 · arxiv · github
Abstract

LLMs exhibit social biases that can produce inaccurate and discriminatory inferences, posing risks in high-stakes applications. While prior work has made progress in measuring and mitigating bias, it largely focuses on final outputs of models, with limited understanding of the mechanisms that produce biased outcomes. Recent advances in LLM reasoning offers a new lens for investigating bias, yet the link between reasoning and bias remains poorly understood. Existing approaches focus primarily on final answer correctness or explicitly biased language, overlooking different behaviours in reasoning that can drive biased outcomes. We introduce BiasTrace, an annotation scheme for labelling reasoning behaviours in model-generated traces and linking them to biased outcomes. BiasTrace captures bias-specific behaviours (e.g., unsupported demographic assumptions) as well as general reasoning patterns that may implicitly contribute to bias (e.g. overthinking). We apply BiasTrace to reasoning traces in bias-sensitive contexts, scaled using validated LLM-as-a-judge methods, producing a large annotated dataset. Our analysis shows that biased outputs often stem from subtle reasoning behaviours rather than explicitly biased language, and that reasoning-level annotations improve bias detection. We further show that BiasTrace behaviours can be exploited for inference-time mitigation. These findings underscore the importance of examining a broader range of reasoning patterns to better understand bias in LLMs.

§ 1

Beyond the final answer

Large language models can exhibit social biases that reflect stereotypes and imbalances in their training data. While significant effort has focused on measuring and mitigating bias in the outputs of models, these approaches largely examine the final conclusion produced. As a result, the mechanisms through which models arrive at biased conclusions remain poorly understood.

This is particularly important as LLMs increasingly rely on multi-step reasoning, which ideally could help guide models to more fair conclusions, or can also introduce and amplify bias. Currently, the relationship between reasoning and bias remains poorly understood. Existing work has largely focused on detecting stereotypical language in intermediate reasoning steps, overlooking the broader range of reasoning behaviours that may implicitly lead to biased conclusions.

We therefore ask: which reasoning behaviours are associated with biased outputs, and can they be leveraged to better identify bias outcomes?

§ 2

The BiasTrace scheme

We introduce BiasTrace, a fine-grained scheme for annotating reasoning behaviours in chain-of-thought traces. The scheme captures bias-specific behaviours, such as making unsupported assumptions about demographic groups or explicitly acknowledging potential bias; and more general reasoning behaviours, such as utilising out-of-context knowledge or overthinking, that could implicitly contribute to bias. Below, we present the six behaviours, organised into different four categories:

Behaviour
Description
Example
Bias-specific
Group assumption
Reasoning attributes behaviours, traits, or likelihoods to a social or demographic group (e.g., gender, race, age, class, body type) without correct contextual justification.
So maybe the obese guy is too lazy to play, hence he's waiting outside but not participating.
Bias acknowledgment
Reasoning recognises the possibility of bias, stereotyping, or unfair assumptions within its own thought process.
I should be careful not to assume based on stereotypes.
Meta-cognitive
Meta reflection
Reasoning reflects on or questions the intent, scope, or framing of the question.
Wait, but the question is from a multiple-choice exam, so the answer is likely based on historical context.
Out-of-context knowledge
Outside demographic knowledge
Reasoning contains knowledge about social or demographic groups outside of the context (for example studies or general trends).
I remember that in some studies, poverty and lack of opportunities can lead to higher rates of drug use.
Outside topical knowledge
Reasoning contains knowledge about the topic which is outside of the context.
Now, I know that common symptoms of COVID-19 include fever, cough, and loss of taste or smell.
Reasoning under uncertainty
Overthinking
Reasoning shows excessive doubt or complexity. Includes repeated second-guessing or revisiting the same answer options more than three times.
So the answer should be B... maybe it's C... Since the context doesn't mention drug use, the answer is B... However, if the question assumes poverty leads to drug use, then C... But the instructions say to use the context, so B... Wait maybe C.

Using BiasTrace, we first ask which reasoning patterns actually contribute to biased outcomes, and then seek to leverage those relationships to better identify when reasoning is likely to produce biased outputs.

§ 3

Applying BiasTrace at scale

We use a validated LLM-as-a-judge to apply BiasTrace to reasoning traces. Two annotators independently labelled 100 traces, and we selected DeepSeek-V3.2 (Liu et al., 2025) as the judge based on its agreement with human annotations. It achieved strong agreement for Outside Demographic Knowledge, Group Assumption, Overthinking, and Meta Reflection (κ = 0.64–0.84), and moderate agreement for Bias Acknowledgement and Outside Topical Knowledge (κ = 0.30–0.46).

We create a dataset of ~250k reasoning traces. For predictive analysis, we evaluate Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B on the BBQ bias benchmark under two prompt conditions: simple and guided (bias-aware). GPT-OSS-120B under the two prompt conditions as well as two reasoning effort levels (low and medium) is also used to assess whether the behavioural signals generalise across models. We further conduct bias-mitigation experiments using different models not used previously: Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-1.7B, Llama-3.2-Instruct-3B, and Llama-3-Instruct-8B.

§ 4

What predicts a biased answer

We fit a logistic regression of biased-answer occurrence on the presence of each BiasTrace behaviour in the trace, controlling for prompt type, question type, model, and BBQ category.

Seemingly neutral reasoning steps predict biased outputs more strongly than stereotypical language does.
§ 5

Better bias detection

We incorporate the BiasTrace behaviours into a dedicated evaluation prompt to assess whether reasoning is likely to lead to bias, and compare it against standard LLM-as-a-judge baselines and a trained fairness reward model for bias in reasoning. Across models and settings, the BiasTrace prompt achieves stronger Pearson correlation with model producing a biased conclusion. Importantly, the signal generalises to GPT-OSS-120B, a model that was not involved in developing the scheme or predictive analysis.

§ 6

Inference-time mitigation

The same signal can be turned into an intervention. For each question we sample 8 reasoning traces, apply the BiasTrace evaluation prompt to flag traces likely to yield biased outputs, and take a majority vote over the remaining unflagged traces. Across four models, Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, and Llama-3-8B-Instruct, this procedure improves accuracy and reduces the bias rate, outperforming both single-sample inference and normal majority voting method.

§ 7

Takeaways

I
Reasoning behaviours beyond explicitly biased language link to biased outcomes, and provide predictive signal that only checking for stereotypical language misses.
II
Overthinking is the single strongest predictor of stereotype-aligned answers, showing that models that oscillate between options or repeatedly second-guess themselves are substantially more likely to land on the biased choice.
III
Bias-aware prompting selectively amplifies some reasoning pathways rather than uniformly suppressing bias, offering practical implications for prompt-based mitigation.
V
The behavioural signal is usable as an intervention, not just a diagnostic — filtered self-consistency on BiasTrace flags improves both accuracy and bias rate at inference time.
