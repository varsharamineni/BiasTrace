BiasTrace Linking reasoning behaviours to biased outputs in LLMs.

Varsha Ramineni, Hossein A. Rahmani, Jerome Ramos, Karin Sevegnani, Emine Yilmaz
University College London · NVIDIA
Read the paper Code & dataset

LLMs can produce inaccurate and discriminatory inferences, posing significant risks in high-stakes applications. When we look at bias in these models, what are we actually looking at — and what are we missing?

01 — The gapBeyond stereotypical language

Existing work on bias in LLMs has largely focused on checking the model's final conclusion, or on checking for stereotypical language in intermediate reasoning steps. As a result, the different mechanisms through which models arrive at biased conclusions remain poorly understood.

A model can produce a stereotype-aligned answer while its reasoning trace reads as careful, self-correcting, and free of overtly biased language. What is actually driving the conclusion?

02 — The schemeIntroducing BiasTrace

We developed BiasTrace, a fine-grained scheme for annotating reasoning behaviours in chain-of-thought traces. Rather than looking only at what the model says, BiasTrace captures how the model reasons — six behaviours grouped into four families: bias-specific reasoning, meta-cognitive reflection, use of out-of-context knowledge, and reasoning under uncertainty.

Overthinking
Excessive doubt, circular reasoning, or repeatedly revisiting the same answer options.
Outside demographic knowledge
Referencing studies, trends, or claims about social groups that aren't in the given context.
Outside topical knowledge
Bringing in subject-matter knowledge beyond what the context provides.
Bias acknowledgement
Recognising within the reasoning that the answer might be biased or stereotypical.
Group assumption
Attributing traits or behaviours to a demographic group without contextual justification.
Meta reflection
Reasoning about the question itself — its framing, intent, or what it's "testing".

03 — Scaling annotationLLM-as-a-Judge, validated

Annotating hundreds of thousands of reasoning traces by hand isn't feasible. We used an LLM-as-a-Judge to scale annotation across the full BBQ benchmark, but validated it carefully against human labels first: two annotators independently labelled 100 traces, and we selected DeepSeek-V3.2 as the judge based on strong agreement with those labels (Cohen's κ of 0.64–0.84 for the main behaviours).

This gave us a dataset of roughly 250,000 annotated reasoning traces across Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and GPT-OSS-120B — enough to actually test which reasoning behaviours predict biased outputs.

04 — The findingNeutral-looking reasoning is the tell

Our main finding: seemingly neutral reasoning steps — like second-guessing answers, or referencing out-of-context demographic studies — predict biased conclusions more strongly than stereotypical language in reasoning does.

When models excessively second-guess themselves under ambiguity — overthinking — they're substantially more likely to land on a stereotype-aligned answer. The effect isn't just verbosity; it holds even after controlling for reasoning length.

Pulling in demographic knowledge from outside the given context is the second-strongest signal, and its effect is stable across prompts and conditions. "Bias-aware" prompting doesn't uniformly help either: it suppresses some pathways while amplifying others (overthinking rises \~10× under bias-aware prompts). And biased outputs cluster in specific combinations of behaviours — overthinking + outside demographic knowledge is a particularly high-risk pattern.

05 — DetectionUsing BiasTrace to spot biased reasoning

These signals aren't just diagnostic — they're actionable. We built a judge prompt that incorporates the BiasTrace behaviours and asks: is this reasoning likely to produce a biased outcome? Across models and prompt conditions, it correlates with biased outputs more strongly than standard LLM-as-a-judge baselines and a trained fairness reward model.

06 — MitigationFairer answers at inference time

Once we can flag reasoning likely to produce a biased conclusion, we can act on it. We sampled multiple reasoning traces per question, filtered out ones flagged as likely-biased by BiasTrace, and majority-voted over what remained. Across Qwen3 and Llama-3 models, this improved both accuracy and bias rate — no fine-tuning required.

07 — TakeawayBias lives in the reasoning

Bias in LLMs isn't fully captured by looking at what models say. Subtle reasoning behaviours — how the model handles uncertainty, what background knowledge it recruits, how it reads the question — carry real signal about where a biased conclusion is coming from.

We hope this encourages more attention to the broader range of mechanisms behind biased outputs, and more evaluation approaches that examine reasoning as well as answers.
