BiasTrace Linking reasoning behaviours to biased outputs
in large language models.

Varsha Ramineni1, Hossein A. Rahmani1, Jerome Ramos1,
Karin Sevegnani2, Emine Yilmaz1
1University College London    2NVIDIA
Preprint · August 2026 · arxiv · github
Abstract

Large language models exhibit social biases that can produce inaccurate and discriminatory inferences, posing risks in high-stakes applications. While prior work has made progress in measuring and mitigating bias, it largely focuses on final outputs of models, with limited understanding of the mechanisms that produce biased outcomes. Recent advances in LLM reasoning offer a new lens for investigating bias, yet the link between reasoning and bias remains poorly understood. Existing approaches focus primarily on final-answer correctness or explicitly biased language, overlooking different behaviours in reasoning that can drive biased outcomes. We introduce BiasTrace, an annotation scheme for labelling reasoning behaviours in model-generated traces and linking them to biased outcomes. Our analysis shows that biased outputs often stem from subtle reasoning behaviours rather than explicitly biased language, and that reasoning-level annotations improve bias detection. We further show that BiasTrace behaviours can be exploited for inference-time mitigation.

§ 1 Beyond the Final Answer

Large language models can exhibit social biases that reflect stereotypes and imbalances in their training data.1 While significant effort has focused on measuring and mitigating bias in the outputs of models, these approaches largely examine the final conclusion produced — a single letter, a single choice, a single sentence. As a result, the mechanisms through which models arrive at biased conclusions remain poorly understood.

1 We use “bias” throughout in the technical sense of stereotype-aligned outputs on the BBQ benchmark, not the broader sociological sense.

This matters more now than it did two years ago. LLMs increasingly rely on multi-step reasoning, and multi-step reasoning is a double-edged tool: it could guide a model to fairer conclusions, or it could introduce and amplify bias along the way.2 Existing work has largely focused on detecting stereotypical language inside intermediate reasoning steps, treating the trace as a bag of sentences to scan for slurs and generalisations. That misses the point. Reasoning is a process, not a lexicon.

2 Prior work sometimes calls this a “reasoning-bias tradeoff”; we find the tradeoff is less about reasoning-vs-no-reasoning and more about which reasoning behaviours occur.

We therefore ask: which reasoning behaviours are associated with biased outputs, and how can identifying them help us better detect and mitigate bias?

§ 2 The BiasTrace Scheme

We introduce BiasTrace, a fine-grained scheme for annotating reasoning behaviours in chain-of-thought traces.3 The scheme captures two kinds of behaviour: bias-specific behaviours, such as making unsupported assumptions about demographic groups or explicitly acknowledging potential bias; and more general reasoning behaviours, such as invoking out-of-context knowledge or overthinking, that could implicitly contribute to bias without ever surfacing as stereotyped language.

3 The taxonomy emerged from close reading of ~500 traces before being formalised. See §3 for how we scaled it via LLM-as-judge.

Six behaviours, organised into four families:

Behaviour
Description
Example
Bias-specific
Group Assumption
Attributing behaviours, traits, or likelihoods to a social or demographic group without correct contextual justification.
So maybe the obese guy is too lazy to play, hence he's waiting outside but not participating.
Bias Acknowledgment
Reasoning that recognises the possibility of bias, stereotyping, or unfair assumptions within its own thought process.
I should be careful not to assume based on stereotypes.
Meta-cognitive
Meta Reflection
Reasoning that reflects on or questions the intent, scope, or framing of the question itself.
Wait, but the question is from a multiple-choice exam, so the answer is likely based on historical context.
Out-of-context knowledge
Outside Demographic Knowledge
Reasoning that references knowledge about social or demographic groups outside of the given context, such as studies or general trends.
I remember that in some studies, poverty and lack of opportunities can lead to higher rates of drug use.
Outside Topical Knowledge
Reasoning that references knowledge about the topic outside of the given context.
Now, I know that common symptoms of COVID-19 include fever, cough, and loss of taste or smell.
Reasoning under uncertainty
Overthinking
Excessive doubt or complexity — repeatedly second-guessing prior reasoning, or revisiting the same answer options more than three times without resolving on new information.
So the answer should be B… maybe it's C. Since the context doesn't mention drug use, the answer is B. However, if the question assumes poverty leads to drug use, then C. But the instructions say to use the context, so B. Wait, maybe C.

Using BiasTrace, we first ask which reasoning patterns actually contribute to biased outcomes, and then leverage those relationships to build better evaluators and mitigations.

§ 3 Applying BiasTrace at Scale

Annotating hundreds of thousands of traces by hand is infeasible; we use a validated LLM-as-a-judge instead. Two annotators independently labelled 100 traces, and we selected DeepSeek-V3.2 as the judge based on strong agreement with those labels — Cohen's κ of 0.64–0.84 across the main behaviours.4

4 Agreement is highest on bt–ot (κ=0.84) and lowest on bt–mr (κ=0.64), which is unsurprising: overthinking has a clean surface signature, meta-reflection often shades into ordinary reasoning.

Using this pipeline we annotate roughly 250,000 reasoning traces generated from Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and GPT-OSS-120B on the BBQ bias benchmark5 — spanning three prompt conditions (unguided, bias-aware, and bias-warning) and two reasoning-effort settings. The full annotated corpus, along with per-trace behaviour distributions, is released with the paper.

5 Parrish et al., BBQ: A Hand-Built Bias Benchmark for Question Answering, ACL 2022. We use the ambiguous-context subset, where a bias-free answer is always available.

§ 4 What Predicts a Biased Answer

We fit a logistic regression of biased-answer occurrence on the presence of each BiasTrace behaviour in the trace, controlling for prompt and model. The result is our main empirical finding.

Seemingly neutral reasoning steps predict biased outputs more strongly than stereotypical language does.
Remark 4.1. The bt–ot effect is robust to a number of alternate specifications: trace length as a covariate, model-fixed effects, and separate regressions per prompt condition all leave the ranking substantially unchanged. Overthinking is not just a proxy for “long trace.”

§ 5 Better Bias Detection

If overthinking and out-of-context appeals genuinely predict biased outputs, they should also serve as features for detection. We incorporate the BiasTrace behaviours into a dedicated evaluation prompt and compare it against standard LLM-as-a-judge baselines and a trained fairness reward model.6 Across models and settings, the BiasTrace prompt achieves stronger Pearson correlation with realised bias — and, importantly, the signal generalises to GPT-OSS-120B, a model that was not involved in developing the scheme.

6 The reward-model baseline is BeaverTails-RM, which is trained on human preference data over harmful/harmless completions rather than bias per se; the LLM-as-a-judge baseline uses the same base model with a generic “rate this reasoning for bias” prompt.

§ 6 Inference-time Mitigation

The same signal can be turned into an intervention. For each question we sample k reasoning traces, apply the BiasTrace evaluation prompt to flag traces likely to yield biased outputs, and take a majority vote over the remaining unflagged traces.7 Across four models — Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, and Llama-3-8B-Instruct — this procedure improves both accuracy and the bias rate simultaneously, outperforming both single-sample inference and vanilla self-consistency.

7 We use k=8 at temperature 0.7. When all k traces are flagged, we back off to unfiltered majority vote — this occurs for roughly 4% of questions.

§ 7 Takeaways

  1. Reasoning behaviours beyond explicitly biased language contribute meaningfully to biased outputs, and provide predictive signal that stereotype-scanning misses.
  2. Overthinking is the single strongest predictor of stereotype-aligned answers — models that oscillate between options or repeatedly second-guess themselves are substantially more likely to land on the biased choice.
  3. Bias-aware prompting selectively amplifies some reasoning pathways rather than uniformly suppressing bias, with practical implications for prompt-based mitigation.
  4. Shifting from final-answer scoring to reasoning-level annotation provides a more interpretable lens on where and why LLMs go biased.
  5. The behavioural signal is usable as an intervention, not just a diagnostic — filtered self-consistency on BiasTrace flags improves both accuracy and bias rate at inference time.
